# Progression Principles and Preference

The [placement structure](/docs/music/voicings-and-placements#placement-structure) laid out how the
LCM-family shapes relate — which placements sit inside which. Those relations are not just a static
map: they suggest *why* certain chords want to move to certain others, and why the ear prefers some
interpretations of an ambiguous set over others.

This chapter turns the structure into three principles — two for progression (**superset** and
**subset**) and one for **preference** — and then shows how several non-LCM scales (dim7, harmonic
minor, blues) live by exploiting them.

## Progression principles

The placement structure shows that an important relationship between LCMs is not only shared lcm
**supersets** but shared lcm **subsets** — e.g. lcm 18 sits below *both* lcm 15 and lcm 24 (the
hexad-A shape feeds both heptads). Such relationships might motivate chord progressions via two
principles:

* **Superset Principle** — Chord A can progress to chord B if A shares an lcm **superset** with B.
* **Subset Principle** — Chord A can progress to chord B if A shares an lcm **subset** with B.

A motivation for the superset principle could be that two subsequent chords are voicings of the same
placement, and placements govern perception. The superset principle has a nice directness to it,
moving straight up/down in the lcm family hierarchy.

A motivation for the subset principle feels less straightforward. Perhaps the brain recognising that
two subsequent chords, while not in the same lcm family, share a subset is good enough. One also
wonders to what extent such subsets are allowed, as there are a lot of dyads which can serve as
shared subsets. Notably lcm 8 and 18 are the big bridging families, and perhaps these subsets are
privileged or preferred when identifying shared subsets.

A concrete example of the **superset** principle for the chord progression C to Eb (4@0 → 4@3):

* C is part of 15@7 (4@0 ≅ 3@7), Eb = 4@3 is part of 18@10.
* Placement 15@7 is an lcm superset of 18@10.
* Both C and Eb then share the superset 15@7 and the superset principle thus allows the progression.

In this way, although `{0 4 7}` (3@7) is not a subset of `{0 3 5 7 8 10}` (18@10) since they do not
share key 4, it is still possible to move to 18@10 since 3@7 is part of its superset lcm family 15@7.

A concrete example of the **subset** principle for the chord progression C to Db (4@0 → 4@1):

* C is part of 15@7 (4@0 ≅ 3@7), Db = 4@1 is part of 24@8.
* Placements 15@7 and 24@8 are both lcm supersets of 18@10.
* The subset principle thus allows a progression from 3@7 (15@7) to 4@1 (24@8) via the shared subset
  18@10.

We should also note what is meant by an *allowed* progression — there is no physical law denying any
two chords from being played one after another. Progressions as motivated by principles instead
guarantee that there is some physically motivated pattern which the brain could find and reward for finding, thus eliciting reproducible emotions.

## Preference

Faced with a multitude of ambiguous interpretations of frequencies, the brain might employ heuristic
preference and collapse competing possibilities under certain circumstances. One such case is the
ambiguity between lcm 24 and 15, where 15@k is adjacent to 24@k+1,k+8 via the shared subset 18@k+3.

As we heard in the [Voicings and LCM Families](/docs/music/voicings-and-lcm-families) section, key
k+8 in 15@k sounds dissonant (the last note in the arpeggio) even though it is technically part of
15@k. This might be because the brain prefers lcm 24 over 15. Stated as a principle:

* **Preference Principle** — Perception favours lcm 24 over 15, choosing the former over the latter
  in ambiguous contexts.

The ear might identify 18@k+3 and try to fit the sounded keys into either 24@k+1 or 24@k+8. Key k+8
may simply be a distinguishing key for 24@k+1,k+8 — perhaps by virtue of being the root or smallest
fraction (perfect fifth, 3/2) of those placements. Key k+8 then collapses 15@k, leaving key k+9 as a
note extraneous to the lcm 24 placements, perceived as a sour or false note.

Perception then might be governed by pragmatic subsets, e.g. lcm 4, 8 and 18, which are expanded with
distinguishing keys to collapse a decision space of possible perceptions. This could be true even for
non-lcm key sets, such as dim7, harmonic minor, blues and the pentatonic scale.

## Double adjacency and non-LCM scales

Several traditional scales are not themselves lcm families, yet sound coherent. A unifying idea is
*double adjacency*: a non-lcm set that sits between two good families by holding the *distinguishing*
key of each neighbour at once, while shedding the key they collapse on. Three well-known sets are
instances, differing only in which families they straddle:

* **dim7** — the symmetric fourfold limit, straddling 24@1,4,7,10 (each dim triad a consonant subset
  of a different family).
* **harmonic minor** — a 15/24 pair (15@2 and 24@3, sharing the subset 18@5).
* **blues** — a 24/24 pair a whole step apart (24@0 and 24@10, via the two thirds).

In each case the residual tension is a dim7, and the "feel" of the scale is the ear swinging between
the co-present interpretations rather than resting in one — these are sets for rapid modulation
between adjacent families.

### Harmonic minor

Harmonic minor harm@k can be thought of as 18@k+5. Consider the adjacent pair 15@2 and 24@3, which
share the subset 18@5:

* 18@5 = `{0 2 3 5 7 10}`
* 15@2 = 18@5 ∪ `{11}` — distinguishing key **11**
* 24@3 = 18@5 ∪ `{8}` — distinguishing key **8**

Harm@0 takes the subset `{0 2 3 5 7 10}` (18@5) and adds **both** distinguishing keys while removing
the collapsing key 10:

harm@0 = `{0 2 3 5 7}` ∪ `{8, 11}` = `{0 2 3 5 7 8 11}`

Key 8 is 24@3's distinguishing note, 11 is 15@2's — so harm@0 plays with the ambiguity between 15@2
and 24@3.

This also explains why harm@0 is less dissonant than plain 15@2: 15@2 still contains the collapse key
10, which forces preference toward 24@3 or 24@10, leaving a sour note 11. Harmonic minor removes 10,
leaving enough ambiguity to not force a sour note.

The set `{0 2 3 5 7}` — 18@5 with key 10 removed — is the **minor pentachord**. It is also the **Jins
Nahawand** in Arabic maqam theory.

### Dim7

A single dim triad dim@k has supersets 24@k+1 and 15@k+3, so the fourfold symmetric dim7 `{0 3 6 9}`
points equally at the four families 24@1,4,7,10 (and 15@0,3,6,9) — each of its four dim triads a
consonant subset of a different family. Adding a distinguishing key (e.g. k+1 with k+10) can focus
perception onto one of them. Perhaps this ambiguity of subsets is why the dim7 sounds both consonant
yet tense.

### Blues

blues@0 = `{0 2 3 4 7}` holds *both* thirds: the major third 4 (the `{0 2 4 7}` ⊆ 8@0 ⊆ 24@0 home)
and the blue third 3 (the `{0 2 3 7}` ⊆ 18@5 ⊆ 24@10). So the blue note is precisely the
distinguishing key that swings the parse into 24@10 while a major triad still asserts 24@0, and the
swing between the two is the blues feel. This is the same dual-distinguishing-key mechanism as in
harmonic minor, but across a pair of lcm 24 families a whole step apart rather than a 15/24 pair.

Concretely, when playing a major chord 0 4 7 the blues note 3 works via a pull to 24@10. Blues@0 is
adjacent to 15@2 which collapses on key 10 — indeed the major chord 7 11 2 using blues note 10 works
well together with 0 4 7 using note 3. There is a similar bluesy feel to a 7 chord e.g. 0 4 7 10,
where the 10 fits the 24@10 pull. Practically this suggests thinking of blues@x as modulating between
8@x+10 and 8@x+3 — the isomorphic groups constituting 24@x+10.

## On composition and perception

Perhaps the larger lcms are not only perceived as themselves, but can be perceived as their
constituents. Certain musical feels or styles could be an exploitation of this ambiguity, e.g. the
swinging motion of blues could be a pull from one perceived placement to another due to ambiguous
interpretations.

Such ambiguity might interact with the human capacity for auditory streams, e.g. the cocktail party
effect and auditory scene analysis. If so, some musical feels are perhaps dependent on having
multiple interpretations and are thus intrinsically complex.

In this way, adjacency is an instructive way of looking at the ambiguity of harmonic structures,
letting us modulate between constituent parts while respecting some seemingly arbitrary preferences
of the musical brain.
