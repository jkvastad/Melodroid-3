# Composition, Adjacency and Preference
Some large lcms are composed of smaller lcms, while some lcms are close in the sense that their keys mostly overlap. 
Such ambigous structures might give rise to a preference for parsing wave patterns and constructing a perception.

## Adjacent key sets and preference
Assuming the brain does not passively parse incoming sounds but rather looks actively for patterns, we would expect preference for certain patterns.
This could mean that some lcms are preferred over others.
Since most lcms are subsets of each other, certain contexts might cause an "obvious" pattern to be ignored in favor of a preferred pattern.
By looking at subsets of lcms we can gain some insight into these possible competing interpretations.
For this purpose we have the subsets subcommand:

```bash
melodroid table subsets --lcm 15 --at 0 --ktet 12
```

### Concluding Remarks
There seems to be a phenomena where there is a large difference of perception between a major vs. minor (or even dim or blues) chord feel when a 15@x placement includes or excludes note x+8.
As we saw in the example for 15@0, key 8 is the root for 8@8 but also for 12@8 (8@1).
Including it in lcm 15 perhaps collapses the perception of the odd pattern to lcm 8 or 24.

#### On Blues
When playing a major chord 0 4 7 the blues note 3 could perhaps work via a pull to 24@10.
Blues@0 is adjacent to 15@2 which collapses on key 10, indeed the major chord 7 11 2 playing blues using key 10 works well together with 0 4 7 using key 3.
There is also a similar bluesy feel of playing a 7 chord e.g. 0 4 7 10 where the 10 fits well with the 24@10 pull feel idea.

Practically this could mean that one should perhaps think blues@x as modulating between 8@x+10,x+3 - the isomorphic groups constituting 24@x+10.

#### On Harmonic Minor
The harmonic minor scale could be the result of ambiguity between 15@x and 24@x+1, similar to blues@x and 24@x+10.
Further, 24@0 is 8@0 composed with 8@5 - a pattern which can be repeated the other way, saying 8@7 composes with 8@0, producing the circle of fifths.

Practically this could mean that playing harm@0 is perhaps best thought of as modulating between 8@3,8,10 - the three isomorphic groups constituting 24@3.
Of note is taking care playing key 10 explicitly as this causes lcm 15 collapse, janking perception from 15 to 8.

#### On composition and perception
Perhaps the larger lcms are not only perceived as themselves, but can be perceived as their consituents. 
Certain musical feels are perhaps an exploitation of this ambiguity, e.g. the swinging motion of blues could be a pull from one perceived placement to another due to ambigous interpretations.

Such ambiguity might interact with the human capacity for auditory streams, e.g. cocktail party effect and auditory scene analysis.
If so, some musical feels are perhaps dependent on having multiple interpretations and are thus intrinsically complex.

In this way, perhaps adjacency is an instructive way of looking at the ambiguity of harmonic structures,
allowing us to modulate between consituent parts,
respecting some seemingly arbitrary preferences of the musical brain.

## High LCMs as compositions of low LCMs
A high lcm family can sometimes be expressed as a composition of lower lcm families.
Since all families $\leq$ 12 are subsets or isomorphisms of each other it seems prudent to use this largest isomorphic group (lcm 8-12) as the building block.
There is some merit to lcm 15 as well, since the the good fractions 15/8 and 16/15 are related to length 15.
This can be explored using the superpositions subcommand:

`melodroid table superpositions --keys 0 2 4 5 7 9 11 --min-block-lcm 8 --max-block-lcm 12`

### LCM 24
Running the superpositions command produces the (truncated) output below for 24@0 = `{0 2 4 5 7 9 11}`

| # | Ref | LCM | Pieces | Union (12-tet) | Extra | Disjoint? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2 | 0 | 24 | 8@0 + 12@0 | 0 2 4 5 7 9 11 | | |
| 2 | 7 | 36 | 9@7 + 12@7 | 0 2 4 5 7 9 11 | | |

From this we see that 24@0 can (at least) be thought of as 8@0 + 12@0 or 9@7 + 12@7. 
In lowest lcm form via isomorphism, it becomes simply 8@0 + 8@5
These pairs of small lcms have overlapping sets: 

8@0 = `{0 2 4 7 11}`, 8@5 = `{5 7 9 0 4 }` $\rightarrow$ 8@0 $\cap$ 8@5 = `{0 4 7}`

Perhaps there is no lcm 24 but instead it is better thought of as these superpositions.
If so, then each 8@x has a neighbour at x+5 (8@0 is in turn 4@0 + 4@7), producing the circle of fifths.


### LCM 20
For 20@0 = `{0 3 4 5 7 8 10}` we note that this is isomprphic to 15@7, but expressed in lcm $\leq$ 12 we have

| # | Ref | LCM | Pieces | Union (12-tet) | Extra | Disjoint? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2 | 0 | 60 | 10@0 + 12@0 | 0 3 4 5 7 8 9 10 | 9 | |

The extra 9 means there is no perfect composition of smaller lcms, perhaps why a full voicing of 20 sounds tense.
Via isomorphism we see that 10@0 + 12@0 becomes 8@8 and 8@5.
Looking at overlapp we see that 

8@8 = `{0 3 7 8 10}`, 8@5 = `{0 4 5 7 9}` $\rightarrow$ 8@8 $\cap$ 8@5 = `{0 7}`

`{0 7}` being the smallest good fraction has quite a lot of possible interpretations. 
If lcms above 12 are compositions of lower lcms then the keys corresponding to a hypothetical lcm 20 might not simply produce anything of particular interest,
 explaining the tense sound of lcm 20 (random noise).

### LCM 18
For 18@0 = `{0 2 5 7 9 10}` we have

| # | Ref | LCM | Pieces | Union (12-tet) | Extra | Disjoint? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2 | 0 | 36 | 9@0 + 12@0 | 0 2 4 5 7 9 10 | 4 | |
| 2 | 5 | 24 | 8@5 + 12@5 | 0 2 4 5 7 9 10 | 4 | |
| 2 | 5 | 36 | 9@5 + 12@5 | 0 2 3 5 7 9 10 | 3 | |
| 2 | 10 | 24 | 8@10 + 12@10 | 0 2 3 5 7 9 10 | 3 | |

Every union carries an extra note (4 or 3), meaning there is no perfect composition of smaller lcms.

The overlaps come in two pairs:

9@0 = `{0 2 5 9 10}`, 12@0 = `{0 4 5 7 9}` $\rightarrow$ 9@0 $\cap$ 12@0 = `{0 5 9}`

8@5 = `{5 7 9 0 4}`, 12@5 = `{5 9 10 0 2}` $\rightarrow$ 8@5 $\cap$ 12@5 = `{0 5 9}`

9@5 = `{5 7 10 2 3}`, 12@5 = `{5 9 10 0 2}` $\rightarrow$ 9@5 $\cap$ 12@5 = `{2 5 10}`

8@10 = `{10 0 2 5 9}`, 12@10 = `{10 2 3 5 7}` $\rightarrow$ 8@10 $\cap$ 12@10 = `{2 5 10}`

Notably lcm 18 and 24 share the same structure: lcm 9 + 12 or 8 + 12 share a root, so perhaps lcm 18 is also better thought of as a superposition of lower lcms.

### LCM 15
For 15@0 = `{0 1 3 5 8 9 10}` we have

| # | Ref | LCM | Pieces | Union (12-tet) | Extra | Disjoint? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2 | 5 | 60 | 10@5 + 12@5 | 0 1 2 3 5 8 9 10 | 2 | |

There is only one composition of two smaller lcms, which also has the best fit but still requiring an extra note (key 2).
In lowest lcm isomorphism form 10@5 + 12@5 becomes 8@1 + 8@10

The overlap is

10@5 = `{5 8 0 1 3}`, 12@5 = `{5 9 10 0 2}` $\rightarrow$ 10@5 $\cap$ 12@5 = `{0 5}`

`{0 5}` is a bare fifth (2@5), again the smallest good fraction — as with lcm 20's `{0 7}` the two pieces barely overlap, perhaps why a full voicing of 15 also sounds tense.

### Concluding remarks
Looking at high LCMs as compositions of low lcms we have the following patterns:

* 24@0 is (perfectly) 
  * 8@0 + 12@0 
  * 9@7 + 12@7
* 20@0 is 10@0 + 12@0 (extra 9)
* 18@0 is 
  * 9@0 + 12@0 (extra 4)
  * 8@5 + 12@5 (extra 4)  
  * 9@5 + 12@5 (extra 3)
  * 8@10 + 12@10 (extra 3)
* 15@0 is 10@5 + 12@5 (extra 2)

From this we see that the structure of 18@0 (adding in the extra key) is identical to 24@5 and 24@10.
Similarly lcm 15 and 20 share structure with 15@0 being 20@5 expressed as 10@5 + 12@5.
With 15 and 20 sounding quite more tense than 24/18, perhaps it is that the total lcm of the composing smaller lcms is much greater for 15/20 (lcm 60) vs. 18/24 with 24 (or 36).
Another possibilty is that 15 is a properly perceived structure, but competes with a preferred alternative (lcm 24), as we will discuss in the upcoming sections.
