# Preference

As we heard in the [Listening to LCM Families](/docs/music/listening-to-lcm-families) section,
lcms with 6+ notes start sounding tense or dissonant, with the exception of lcm 18 being the minor 11 chord.

We also heard that harmonic minor, in a sense a modified lcm 15, produces better/more coherent melodies than pure lcm 15.
Similarly the major blues also produced nice melodies despite not being a proper lcm family.

This raises some questions:

* Why does the other hexad, lcm 20, not sound consonant?
* Why is the lcm 24 heptad more consonant than the lcm 15 heptad?
* Why do blues and harmonic minor produce coherent melodies without being proper lcm sets?

Looking back at [the placement structure](/docs/music/placements) we see that for any given note, there are plenty of possibilities for which placement it belongs to.
Faced with such a multitude of ambiguous interpretations of frequencies, the brain might employ heuristic preference and collapse competing possibilities.

## The principle of preference
A prime absent from both lcm 18 and 24 is the largest good fraction prime 5. 
One way to decide which placement to perceive is to avoid using it.
Such a bias would lead to a principle when parsing sound for music:

* **Preference Principle** — Perception prefers small primes in ambiguous contexts.

Lets look at 15@11, displayed as a leaf node in the extended triad placement graph.

When playing 15@11, the preference principle could make the brain perceive it as 18@2.
Since 18@2 has supersets of 24@0,7, an attempt to play 15@11 might be perceived as playing 18@2 or 24@0,7 but with bad notes - namely key 8 which is exclusive to 15@11.

Indeed if we play melody over 15@11 then key 7 seems to be a sour note, producing a dissonant experience, even though it is part of 15@11.
The player below has fixed melodies per chord and scale to showcase this.

Let's introduce the stable lcm 15 scale 15s, which is lcm 15 without 8/5 (key 7 in 15@11). 
The melodies for 15s@11 and 15@11 are identical except that key 8 is changed for 7 in 15@11.
We also have a similar melody for harm@9 which is 15s@11 with an added key 5.
The blues melody starts of like the others but then uses the blues exclusive key 1.

With a major chord accompaniment 15s@11, harm@9 and blues@4 sound good but key 7 in 15@11 sounds like it is pulling into the blues scale, creating a confused/dissonant feeling.

With a minor chord accompaniment 15s@11 and harm@9 still sound good, 15@11 sounds better but blues@4 is now dissonant, likely because key 0 is not in blues 4.

This effect could perhaps be due to 9 0 4 having a superset of 8@5 which naturally includes key 7, while 4 8 11 must resolve to 15@11 or its subset 20@4 to fit in key 7.

** deferred/tentative below **

Notably key 7 is the root of 24@7 and the dyad for 24@0.
The dyad for 24@7 is key 2, which sounds good with 15@11.
The root for 24@0 is key 0, which sounds good with 15@11.
Both key 0 and 2 provide the characteristic oriental feel of lcm 15.
Interestingly key 5 fits well with the other keys of 15@11, producing the harmonic minor at 9.
Key 5 is part of both 24@0,7 but not 15@11.

Notable features:
Perhaps by being the dyad in 8@0 key 7 collapses 15@11?
Both lcm 18 and 24 lack a prime factor of 5.
lcm 18 lacks a symmetric subset; 0 4 8 in 15 and 20; 0 3 6 in 15, 20, 24.

... one such case is the ambiguity between lcm 24 and 15, where 15@k is adjacent to 24@k+1,k+8 via the shared subset 18@k+3.

As we heard in the [Listening to LCM Families](/docs/music/listening-to-lcm-families) section, key
k+8 in 15@k sounds dissonant (the last note in the arpeggio) even though it is technically part of
15@k. This might be because the brain prefers lcm 24 over 15:

* **Preference Principle** — Perception favours lcm 24 over 15, choosing the former over the latter
  in ambiguous contexts.

The ear might identify 18@k+3 and try to fit the sounded keys into either 24@k+1 or 24@k+8. Key k+8
may simply be a distinguishing key for 24@k+1,k+8 — perhaps by virtue of being the root or smallest
fraction (perfect fifth, 3/2) of those placements. Key k+8 then collapses 15@k, leaving key k+9 as a
note extraneous to the lcm 24 placements, perceived as a sour or false note.

Perception then might be governed by pragmatic subsets, e.g. lcm 4, 8 and 18, which are expanded with
distinguishing keys to collapse a decision space of possible perceptions. This could be true even for
non-lcm key sets, such as dim7, harmonic minor, blues and the pentatonic scale.
