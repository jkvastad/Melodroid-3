# Preference

As we heard in the [Listening to LCM Families](/docs/music/listening-to-lcm-families) section,
lcms with 6+ notes start sounding tense or dissonant, with the exception of lcm 18 being the minor 11 chord.

We also heard that harmonic minor, in a sense a modified lcm 15, produces better/more coherent melodies than pure lcm 15.
Similarly the major blues also produced nice melodies despite not being a proper lcm family.

This raises some questions:

* Why does the other hexad, lcm 20, not sound consonant?
* Why is the lcm 24 heptad more consonant than the lcm 15 heptad?
* Why do blues and harmonic minor produce coherent melodies without being proper lcm sets?

Looking back at [the placement structure](/docs/music/placements) we see that for any given note, there are plenty of possibilities for which placement it belongs to.
Faced with such a multitude of ambiguous interpretations of frequencies, the brain might employ heuristic preference and collapse competing possibilities.

## The principle of preference
A prime absent from both lcm 18 and 24 is the largest good fraction prime 5. 
A possible heuristic for deciding which placement to perceive is to favor placements with small primes.
This could be motivated by the brain favoring not just patterns with small WPL, but patterns with small subpatterns to chunk up.
Such behavior would be in line with having WPLs on the larger side, e.g. 15 and 24, as their constituent primes might be the bottleneck.
It would also explain why lcm 15 and 20 sound more dissonant than 24, since they carry the large prime 5.

The bias stated as a principle when parsing sound for music:

* **Preference Principle** — Perception prefers placements with small primes.

Keys not covered by the preferred placement, or key sets completely lacking any covering placement, 
will likely sound sour or wrong while covered keys sound good or right.
Such feedback would establish a sort of communication protocol for humans, sending data on certain channels (placements), which can have evolutionary benefits.

The preference principle will be of particular interest in competing contexts.
Lets look at 15@11, displayed as a leaf node in the extended triad placement graph.

### 15 vs 24
When playing 15@11, the preference principle could make the brain perceive it as 18@2.
Since 18@2 has supersets of 24@0,7, an attempt to play 15@11 might be perceived as playing 18@2 or 24@0,7 but with bad notes - namely key 8 which is exclusive to 15@11.

Indeed if we play melody over 15@11 the experience turns dissonant with key 7, even though it is part of 15@11.
The player below has fixed melodies per chord and scale to showcase this
(effects are more noticeable with the piano instrument than with the sine instrument).

Let's introduce the stable lcm 15 based placement 15s@0 = `{0 1 3 5 9 10}`, which is lcm 15 without 8/5 (key 7 in 15@11). 
The melodies for 15s@11 and 15@11 are identical except that key 8 is changed for 7 in 15@11.
We also have a similar melody for harm@9 which is 15s@11 with an added key 5.
The blues melody starts of like the others but then uses the blues exclusive keys 1 and 6.

With a major chord accompaniment 15s@11, harm@9 and blues@4 sound good but key 7 in 15@11 sounds like it is pulling into the blues scale, creating a confused/dissonant feeling.

With a minor chord accompaniment 15s@11 and harm@9 still sound good, 15@11 sounds better but blues@4 is now dissonant, likely because neither key 0 or 9 is in blues 4.

In summary:

| Placement | Major (4 8 11) | Minor (9 0 4) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15s@11 | good | good |
| 15@11 | bad | good |
| blues@4 | good | bad |
| harm@9 | good | good |

Notably when key 7 is played to imply 15@11 the major chord accompaniment sounds bad but the minor chord sounds good.

Here we should take care with causation and correlation: playing key 7 correlates with a dissonant perception, but key 7 is perhaps not the offender:
* The placement 15@11 is adjacent to 18@2 and via it closely related to 24@0 and 24@7. 
* All of these related placements lack key 8, but are also free of prime 5.

The brain might at first be perceiving the sounds played as 15@11, but when key 7 is introduced the principle of preference forces perception of a placement without key 8 - 
leaving the major chord 4 8 11 dissonant with its key 8 suddenly an extraenous key.
Since 9 0 4 does not have a key 8 and is a subset of 18@2 it has no problem with playing key 7.

### Why only key 7?
Why does collapse depend on the chord being played, and why don't other keys of 15@11 collapse perception?

Tabulating each key as the fraction it represents in 15@11 and in the three prime-5-free relatives:

One could expect that 24@0,7 played as a block would produce a similar feeling, but it seems the voicing is important:

* 24@0 from major sounds consonant
* 24@0 from minor is dissonant at key 17
* 24@7 from major is dissonant at key 12
* 24@7 from minor is consonant
