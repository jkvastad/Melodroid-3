# Melody, Rhythm and Chords

To play melodies we will need to move from playing block chords in static time into dynamic time. 
This produces structures of beats implicit in the change of notes (see [Rhythm chapter](/docs/related-research/rhythm) for related research and basic concepts).

## Rhythmic Patterns
Rhythmic patterns can be constructed by:

* Selecting a meter consisting of a series of integers e.g. `{4}` or `{7,2,3}`. These numbers signify the heavy accents on beats in the reccuring pattern.
* Selecting a tempo (beats per minute, bpm) by choosing the rate of the (possibly implicit) unit beat. 
Since meters come in integer ratios there is always a unit beat e.g. `{4}={4,1}`.
* Selecting a grid resolution or fastest pulse by choosing the largest division of the unit beat. 
This subdivision is typically in the range of 1-8 since maximum groove is about 100-120bpm, producing a fastest pulse of 62.5ms. 
Any faster and the pulse starts intruding on the pitch domain.
* Accenting pulses (possibly silent) in the pattern. This decides long, heavy, light, short, moderate, etc., note amplitude and length.
When accenting pulses a moderate deviation from the meter produces the highest groove.

The above procedure produces an amplitude pattern consisting of meter and rhythm. 
This pattern can then be decorated with pitch at active (non-silent) pulses.

Try it below. Enter a **meter** (one number per accent
group, e.g. `4` or `7 2 3`) and a **subdivision** (a single number for the whole meter,
or one per group), then shape the pattern with two sliders:

* **Resolution** — how much of the pulse grid is active. At the low end only the main
beats fire; at the high end every subdivision does.
* **Syncopation** — deviation from the meter. At zero the heavy accents sit exactly on
the meter beats; as it rises the accents redistribute, drifting onto off-beat pulses
while some meter beats drop to lighter accents.

Press **Generate** to generate a concrete pattern from the current settings, and **Play** to loop it. The plot shows each pulse's velocity
against its position in unit beats, with grid lines for the meter accents (bold), unit
beats (medium), and the full pulse grid (faint). **Tempo** adjusts live while playing.

The **phrase** field builds a song-like structure by repeating the rhythm of earlier metric
groups. Write one token per meter group: an uppercase letter is that group's identity, and
reusing a letter makes that group a full copy of the first group carrying it. So under a four-group
meter, `ABAC` restates group 1's rhythm in group 3, while groups 2 and 4 stay original. A trailing
lowercase letter repeats only the *first half* of another group: `A Ba A Ca` (equivalently
`ABaACa`) keeps groups 2 and 4 opening with group 1's first half before diverging. A full repeat
needs only the source and target groups to share a subdivision — the lengths may differ, and the
rhythm best-fits: a shorter target takes the source's opening (a 2 copying a 4 takes its first
half), while a longer target tiles the source, repeating as much as fits. A half repeat still needs
an identical source (same length and subdivision) and an even length. Leave the field blank for a
fresh, independent rhythm in every group.

## Melody
A naive approach to lcm melody is to randomly select pitch values from an lcm family to decorate a rhythmic pattern.

The player below adds an lcm dropdown listing the LCM families from the table at the top of the page. 
Pulses are pitched with a note drawn at random from the selected family's notes, folded into a single octave.
Each bar is tinted along the visible spectrum by the pitch it carries — low
pitch (long wavelength) red, high pitch blue/violet. 

**Loop melody** bakes the drawn pitches into a repeating phrase; turn it off to re-roll a fresh pitch on every hit.
 **Generate** samples a new rhythm and a new melody to go with it.

 You can also listen to a chromatic scale, true random pitch, two familiar tonal scales (major pentatonic and harmonic minor), and the symmetric chords that divide the octave evenly (whole tone, diminished 7th, augmented) for comparison.
 The **instrument** dropdown switches both the melody and the chord between the theory-faithful sines and a sampled piano.[^salamander]

<RhythmPatternPlayer meter="4" subdivisions="2" bpm={100} melody />

Anecdotally the random pitches and chromatic scale sound quite similar, a quality perhaps best described as chaotic, psychadelic or confusing.
This quality is shared to some degree with the symmetric chords and harmonic minor. 

Lcm families up to and including 12 instead sound pleasant/soft/round/free of tension, a quality shared with the major pentatonic scale (subset of 24@0 among others). 
Lcms 15 and above produce more tension, or perhaps randomness. 
This is especially true for lcm 15 and 20 with sound reminiscent of the random/chromatic pitches.

Notably the default tones used here are sines and do not sound wrong in and of themselves (no overtones to produce roughness).
Playing piano notes instead produces much the same sensatinos as sines, notably the truly random pitches sound even more pronounced with a metalic quality.

Where does the chaotic sound come from? There are at least two flavors - random/tense and harmonious/soft. 
Perhaps it comes from context - the listener can expect certain pitches from preceding notes (or even having heard/recognizing a melody beforehand), or from pattern matching of wavepackets.
A common context in music is chords - a set of notes played statically along with the melody.

## Chords
Let's now look at chords. In their most general form chords are two or more simultaneous notes.
In the 12 tet system there are $2^{12} = 4096$ possible key combinations per octave, including the empty set.
In particular we are interested in unique chords under octave renormalization where e.g. 0 4 7, 0 3 8 and 0 5 9 are the same.

Counting a chord and all its transpositions once collapses the 4096 combinations to just 352 unique chords (the [binary necklaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necklace_(combinatorics)) of length 12),
 or 280 across a more pragmatic 2–7 note range.
These can be enumerated with the `chords` subcommand:

```bash
melodroid table chords --min-notes 2 --max-notes 7
```

We note that most of these chords do not sound particularly good, containing a minor second or tritone. 
Removing minor seconds produces 29 chords.
Removing tritones produces 61 chords.
Removing both produces 16 chords.

There are however some chords which do have a nice sounding minor second (m2) of sorts - a major seventh (M7) in e.g. 0 4 7 11 14 (lcm 8@0) sounds quite nice, even if the interval 11 does not.

Interestingly, playing first 0 4 7 11 and then playing 0 11, the 0 11 keeps the rounder/softer quality of the preceding chord. 
Follow this up with 1 12 to produce a harsh m2/M7 which then becomes soft by adding in the related 8@1 i.e. 1 5 8 12. 
This process can be repeated with e.g. 2 13 and so on:

Such an effect hints that it matters not just what we hear, but what we recently heard. 
How recent is recent? Perhaps it is simply what we expect to hear, heavily influenced by what we just heard, but also by other expectations such as familiar melodies and songs.
This calls to mind the McGurk effect, an effect where context affects perception (see e.g. [this BBC clip](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2k8fHR9jKVM) illustrating how "Ba" turns into "Fa" when seeing lips produce "Fa", even though the audio is still "Ba").

There are also chords with tritones which sound good. These are primarily dim (0 3 6) and chords containing it e.g. dim7 (0 3 6 9) and seven chords (e.g. 0 4 7 10).
Perhaps the characteristic dramatic sound stems from the symmetric dim7 chord.

### Chords as placement indicators
Another way to look at chords from HPT is as indicators for lcm family placements. 
The idea is that the brain tries to match sound to a root and lcm family, and that this heavily influences our perception of harmony.

Looking at the major chord 0 4 7 we have 23 possible placements containing this chord, including 4@0, 8@0 and 24@0. 
Looking at the minor chord 0 3 7 we have 12 possible placements containing this chord, including 10@0, 15@2, 8@8 and 24@8.

While these chords are subsets of lcm families, some of their possible placements are in turn also supersets of each other (e.g. 4@0, 8@0 and 24@0 or 10@0 and 24@8). 
Some chord supersets are also isomorphic, such as the minor chords 10@0 and 8@8.

This raises several questions about what to expect of placement based perception:
* Do we hear the predicted top superset, e.g. instead of 4@0 we hear 24@0?
  * If so, what are the top supersets? As we saw earlier, high lcms such as 24 can be composed by lower lcms such as 8 and 12.
* Do we hear a single placement or several?
  * If several, is our perception an aggregation of possible interpretations? Is it more akin to the cocktail party effect where some parts receive focused? Is it a mix?
  * If only one, how does the brain pick one placement over competing placements? (Especially for isomorphisms)
* What about perceptions which do not seem to map to placements?
  * Symmetric chord such as aug and dim7 have a characteristic sound but no one obvious placement due to symmetry - aug also fits with the whole tone scale which has a unique dreamy/confused quality.

The number of possible chords from a placement indicator perspective is similar to the combinatorial necklaces discussed above.
This time instead of all possible key combinations in a k-tet, we require a chord to be a subset of at least one lcm family.
This means all binary necklaces with an lcm family:

```bash
melodroid table chords --lcm-only --max-lcm 24
```

With the default `--max-lcm 24` there are 102 chords across 2–7 notes. 
Notably only the tritone `0 6` has no low-LCM placement — it is only a subset of the lcm 15/20/24 families. 
Restricting to the isomorphic ≤12 group therefore drops it and collapses the set from 102 to 20 chords.

#### Placement indicators
Looking at a select number of chords as placement indicators we can get a feel for how they might produce perception.

For instance, the major chord 0 4 7 which is the placement 4@0 is also a subset of 8@0 and 10@4. The latter contains 4 7 11, a minor chord of 10@4. 
These have typical major/minor flavor but also work well in series, hinting at a mechanism for harmonic progressions. 

The simplest chord in 12-tet is perhaps the minor second 0 1 with only two notes and the smallest separation. 
As noted above when looking at major sevenths, which in a sense are just renormalised minor seconds, they can sound quite harsh on their own but adding in context softens the dyad.

Why does note 2 sound bad in a 15@6 context when playing a major chord, but works with 4 7 11? 
Perhaps the 2 with 4 7 11 gets parsed as 8@7 - this is no problem with 4 7 11, but via preference might invalidate 15@6 in favor of 8@7 or 24@7, causing dissonance.

The minor chord seemingly works with both blues@11 and blues@7.

Neither the blues scale nor these extensions of it are direct lcm families. 
Perhaps it is an effect of preference, or perhaps one of composition, where the scale is actually modulating between multiple smaller lcms scale.
Another possibility is that of fusion and separation, where fusion refers to multiple tones fusing into one.
Since the auditory system is capable of separating both multiple conversations and merge tones, 
it might be that playing complex music e.g. blues scales with accompaniment is a perception arising from interplay of fusing and separating placements.

### Chords as accompaniment
In the context of accompaniment, for a given chord we must choose a set(s) of notes from which to draw melody.

This presents us with two questions - how do we choose chords and how do we choose the corresponding melody set?
For constructing chords we have several options:

* Random notes
* A subset to an lcm
* Symmetric chords, possibly a subset of larger symmetric structures (e.g. the whole tone scale)

In HPT the only governing structure is the lcms of the constituent good fractions. 
Choosing random chords should thus either produce lcm subsets by luck or no structure at all.
Symmetric chords are notable because of their ambiguity: it is not the lcm structure in and of itself which is ultimately interesting but our perception of the structure.

For constructing a melody set we also have several options:

* Random notes
* The notes constituting the chord
* Notes from an lcm for which the chord is a subset (full matches)

We previousy listened to random notes and observed they have a specific almost chaotic quality.
Melodic notes constituting a chord will likely inherit its qualities from whatever structure the chord has.
Notes from an lcm should similarly produce soft/harmonious melodies from lcms $\leq$ 12.

Notably a chord can be a subset to several lcms simultaneously and a union or intersection of such shared lcms might be an interesting melodic set,
providing some form of modulation between lcms.

The largest lcm family consist of 7 members, which is also large enough to include the whole tone scale and the harmonic minor.
We can thus create chords by selecting between 2 and 7 notes. Below we do so by randomly choosing chords until they have at least one placement.

#### Scales and lcms
As we noted in the discussion on high lcms as compositions of lower lcms, and in the section on placement indicators,
some high lcm scales sound harsh/random (15, 20), while some scales with no placement sound fine (harmonic minor, blues scale).

In addition we also noted that the blues scale could be extended with a minor 7 for melody if the accompaniment was a major chord, and with a major 7 if the accompaniment was a minor.
Such an effect, and the effects of context on e.g. the minor second/major seven duality suggests that perhaps som sort of expectation or parsing preference is at play.

Given a chord with multiple possible placements we can list all supersets which contain it. 
Conversely, given a scale we can find all placements which compose it.
Further, some placements are close or adjacent in the sense that dropping a key from the set shifts it to a subset of another placement, e.g. dropping key 9 from 15@0 makes it a subset of both 24@1 and 24@8.

Let's now look at some non lcm scales which sound good, and some lcm scales which sound bad. We can then ask 

* Is the scale bad despite being lcm because it is preferred by another set of notes, causing notes which should sound good become wrong? I.e. hidden lcms.
* Is the scale good despite not being lcm because it is composed of multiple lower order lcms, and playing melody in it rapidly modulates between these compatible lcms?
* If neither of the above, is there some special circumstance such as symmetric chords?

##### LCM 15
We noted earlier that 15@0 is adjacent to 24@1 and 24@8 via removal of key 9. 
We also noted that 24@0 is a composition of 8@0 + 12@0 or 9@7 + 12@7, or alternatively in lowest lcm form via isomorphism, simply 8@0 + 8@5.

Writing 24@1 and 24@8 this way produces the three families 8@1,6,8.
These families share keys 1 5 8 (4@1).

If note 8 sounds bad in 15@0 due to preference, then this leaves a stable set 15s@0 of 0 1 3 5 9 10.
Playing triads from this stable set produces quite a different feel from playing such triads from 24@1,8.

## Melodic Supersets
Several of the scales above which are not lcms scales are compositions of two lcm scales, or is heavily related to a symmetric chord.
This provides a possibility that scales are perhaps best thought of as melodic sets of sets, where a chord provides some sort of context and the melody modulates between possible interpretations of the context.

Earlier we looked at placement indicators, as well as lcm preference in the context of lcm 15.
Perhaps there is also a preference for how chords are interpreted, producing the well known character/flavor of e.g. major/minor/dim chords.
