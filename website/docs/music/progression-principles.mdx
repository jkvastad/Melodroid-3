# Progression Principles

Thus far we have focused on static harmony, i.e. various block chords, with limited melody.
Let's now turn to more dynamic harmony using sequences of notes such as chord progressions.

While any two sets of notes can be played one after another, some seem to fit better together.
Surely there are reasons behind phenomena such as cadences, circles of fifths and 3-chord-songs (e.g. C, F, G). 
If we can define principles based on physical features which recover popular progressions, 
we might glean some insight into the inner workings of music and then extrapolate from those principles to aid in composition.

## Placement Progressions

The concept of progression requires time, and time in music seems to consist of discrete pulses, as discussed in [related research on rhythm.](/docs/related-research/rhythm)
Progressions in music then refer to the relationships of sets of notes at consecutive pulses, and various groupings thereof.

A natural relationship of consecutive notes is frequency invariance: notes can be completely random or remain constant.
In the middle of such a spectrum we have a morphing of notes where some notes stay the same while some change.

In HPT we discuss relationships based on harmonic patterns. 
Some sets of keys map to small repeating patterns (full matches with low WPL). 
Changing or morphing such a key set can be illustrated as walks in a placement graph (adding or removing a key from a placement is a step in the graph).
Such walks are most generally made in the global placement graph. 
The global placement graph consists of all the twelve extended tonic placement graphs of 12-TET, allowing walks between any two placements.
Such a walk where origin and target shares at least one key motivates the idea of a placement progression:

* A **Placement Progression** maps the notes at one pulse to a placement A, the notes of a consecutive pulse to a placement B, and bridges them via a placement S in the global placement graph.

Thus a placement progression can be written as a triplet consisting of the origin, bridge and target placements containing the notes of two successive pulses.
Note that origin, bridge and target can all be the same placement, e.g. three chord song C F G can be seen as movements entirely within 24@0.

### Examples
**C to Eb (4@0 → 4@3)**

* C is part of 15@7 (4@0 ≅ 3@7)
* Eb = 4@3 is part of 18@10
* Placement 15@7 is an lcm superset of 18@10.
* C -> Eb is then the triplet 15@7 -> 18@10 -> 18@10, where the bridge is also the target.

In this way, although `{0 4 7}` (3@7) is not a subset of `{0 3 5 7 8 10}` (18@10) since they do not
share key 4, it is still possible to move to 18@10 since 15@7 is a superset of 18@10.

Notably 4@3 is also part of 15@7, but since key 3 collapses 15@7 it is not a stable target.
While not useful for sustaining melody or chords, the collapsing key still allows us to go to 18@10 when progressing chords.

## Composite Scales

In the preference chapter we touched on the non-lcm scales of blues and harmonic minor, which produce
coherent melodies while being neither lcm sets nor subsets. 
As we shall see these turn out to be composite scales — ambigous unions of adjacent placements.

Using the [`table composite-scales`](/docs/cli/reference#composite-scales) command we can enumerate composite scales.

Looking at large shared subsets with the `--min-subset-notes 6` option yields some familiar scales:

```
Scale                         Size  P_A·S·P_B
{0, 1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10}          7   15@0·18@3·24@1     ← harmonic minor
{0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 10}          7   15@0·18@3·24@8     ← melodic minor
{0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 11}      8   24@0·18@7·24@5     ← bebop dominant (contains blues)
```

Each row is a concrete 12-tet key set with its size and the `P_A·S·P_B` constituents that produced it. 
The rows have been stabilized with the `--stable` option which removes collapsing keys from the final sets.
For example `15@0·18@3·24@8` contains no key 8 because it collapses the constituent `15@0`.

In the table we see two classical minor scales — **harmonic minor** and **melodic minor**.
We also see the **bebop dominant** scale, which contains blues as a subset.

Investigating smaller bridges produces the harmonic major at `--min-subset-notes 4`. 
Going lower still produces no obvious traditional scales.

### Harmonic minor as a composite scale
Looking at the first row of the intro table we have: `P_A = 15@0 = {0 1 3 5 8 9 10}` and
`P_B = 24@1 = {0 1 3 5 6 8 10}`. They share the subset `S = 18@3 = {0 1 3 5 8 10}` — their common
parent. Their union is

$$
15@0 \cup 24@1 = \{0\;1\;3\;5\;6\;8\;9\;10\}
$$

This however includes the collapse key `8` of `15@0`. 
Removing it produces harmonic minor with root 10:

$$
15@0 \cup 24@1 \;\setminus\; \{8\} \;=\; \{0\;1\;3\;5\;6\;9\;10\} \;=\; \mathrm{harm}@10.
$$

### Melodic minor as a composite scale

From the extended triad placement graph we see that 15@k relates to 24@(k+1), 24@(k+8) and 18@(k+3).
At `k = 0` this gives `15@0` two `lcm-24` partners sharing the same `18@3` parent. 
Harmonic minor used `24@1` (the `k+1` partner). The `k+8` partner `24@8` is the sibling composite scale:

$$
15@0 \cup 24@8 \;\setminus\; \{8\} \;=\; \{0\;1\;3\;5\;7\;9\;10\} =\mathrm{mel}@10
$$

This set is the **melodic minor** (ascending). So the two `lcm-24` partners of `15@0` hand us the
two classical minor scales — harmonic minor via `24@1`, melodic minor via `24@8`.

### Bebop dominant and blues

Take `P_A = 24@0 = {0 2 4 5 7 9 11}` and `P_B = 24@5 = {0 2 4 5 7 9 10}`, sharing `S = 18@7 = {0 2 4 5 7 9}`:

$$
24@0 \cup 24@5 = \{0\;2\;4\;5\;7\;9\;10\;11\} = \mathrm{bebop}@0
$$

This is the [**bebop dominant**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebop_scale#Bebop_dominant_scale) — a major scale with an added flat seventh.
Similarly to harmonic minor and melodic minor, removing the fifth and root of 24@5 produces a traditional scale, namely the blues major scale:

$$
24@0 \cup 24@5 \;\setminus\; {0\;5} = \{\;2\;4\;7\;9\;10\;11\} = \mathrm{blues}@7
$$

This echoes our subset inspections in the preference chapter, where blues@4 was closely tied to 24@9, and here we see blues@7 tied to 24@0, repeating the pattern of a 5 key root difference.

The removed root notes are also roots of 8@0,5 which sit as branching points for two sub trees with a shared leaf node of 24@0.
Perhaps this ambivalence between two closely adjacent lcm families is the source of the swingy sound.

### Harmonic major as a composite scale

When allowing smaller bridges with `--min-subset-notes 4` we find the **harmonic major**. 

Take `P_A = 15@0 = {0 1 3 5 8 9 10}`
and `P_B = 15@5 = {1 2 3 5 6 8 10}`. They share

$$
S = 5@5 = \{1\;3\;5\;8\}
$$

Their union is

$$
15@0 \cup 15@5 = \{0\;1\;2\;3\;5\;6\;8\;9\;10\}
$$

This looks similar to the bebop dominant and the blues.
Instead of mixing two lcm 24 placements from two different branches (8@0,5) we mix two lcm 15 placements from the same distinct branches but less directly since we come from the tetrad instead of the hexad.

This time however both sides are `lcm-15`, so both collapse keys drop — key `8` from `15@0` and key `1` from
`15@5`:

$$
15@0 \cup 15@5 \;\setminus\; \{1,\;8\} \;=\; \{0\;2\;3\;5\;6\;9\;10\} \;=\; \mathrm{hmaj}@10
$$

Where harmonic minor drops a single collapse key from a one-sided collapse,
harmonic major drops one from each side of a symmetric `lcm-15 · lcm-5 · lcm-15` composite scale.

Loosening the floor further to `--min-subset-notes 3` admits bare triad bridges, but these only
generate 8-, 9- and 10-note chromatic clusters that aren't obvious traditional scales, so we leave them aside.

### Composite scales on the placement graph

Every composite scale above is two placements sharing a subset `S`, and `S` is exactly the node where the two meet walking down the [extended triad placement graph](/docs/music/placements). 
Sorted by how deep the shared bridge sits in the graph:

| Scale | Constituents | Bridge node |
| --- | --- | --- |
| harmonic minor | `15@11 · 18@2 · 24@0` | `18@2` hexad |
| melodic minor | `15@11 · 18@2 · 24@7` | `18@2` hexad |
| bebop dominant | `24@0 · 18@7 · 24@5` | `18@7` hexad |
| harmonic major | `15@11 · 5@4 · 15@4` | `5@4` tetrad |

Notably harmonic and melodic minor, as well as the bebop dominant, are pairwise combinations of the leaf nodes of 18@2.
Harmonic major instead mixes lcm 15 from two different lcm 18 nodes, requiring the deeper bridge of 5@4.

Common to all of these is also a necessity for ambiguity by removing certain distinguishing notes:

* Harmonic minor removes the fifth from 24@0, the distinguishing key between 1@0 and 2@0 and thus the earliest such key in the extended tonic placement graph.
This is also another reason why lcm 15 collapses in addition to being expensive by containing prime 5 - 
its key set is quite close to lcm 24 and the earliest branch in that subtree from the tonic is the dyad created with the perfect fifth.
* Melodic minor removes the tonic from 24@7 while also stabilising 15@11
* Bebop dominant sounds quite bluesy in and of itself, and especielly when omitting the lcm 24 roots 0 and 5, recovering the major blues scale.
Notably the minor seven chord is present in bebop, and its strong pull to the perfect fourth (C7 -> F) could be due to the composition of the scale.
* Harmonic major removes both collapsing keys from its lcm 15 constituents.

## Composite Chords
From harmonic major and minor we saw the symmetric chord dim7@0 = `{0 3 6 9}`. 
This chords is of special interest due to its perfect symmetry, and its popular usage despite not being contained by any placement.

Composite scales in general host key sets that lie in no placement at all, but are useful in composition.
This requires an expansion of the placement progression principle to include composite key sets.

## Composite Placement Progression

The placement progression principle handles note sets which are contained by placements. 
Having discussed composite scales and chords, which are not necessarily in the global placement graph, we need a generalised principle for describing composite progressions.

* A **Composite Placement Progression** maps the notes at one pulse to a composite scale, the notes of a
  consecutive pulse to another composite scale, and links them by a *placement progression between
  their constituent placements*.

Progression between composite scales is therefore also a walk in the global placement graph, but
run between two composite note sets constituents rather than between two proper placements. 
The plain placement progression is the degenerate case where each composite is a single placement — a one-constituent
composite scale.

## Example Chord Progressions
Using the `progression` command we can enumerate all possible progressions between two chords.
Let's use this to analyze some chord sequences.

### C F G
A very common "[three chord song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-chord_song)", C F G is the I, IV, V progression of the major scale.

Expressed in placements, using key 0 for key c, the major scale is 24@0 with C=4@0, F=4@5 and G=4@7. 
Looking at the progressions between the chords we find:
* C=`0 4 7` and F=`0 5 9` are both contained by 8@5.
* F and G=`2 7 11` are contained by 24@0. (Interesting subsets include e.g. 8@5->4@0->8@0 and 18@7->24@0 which move between the 8@0 and 8@5 branch.)
* G and C are in 8@0.

Given these observations the main mechanism might be the superset principle within lcm 8 which contains two major triads, explaining the I->IV and V->I movement. 
The IV->V is a larger move, either seen as a superset principle move withing 24@0 (which contains all the three chords), or as a subset principle move shifting the chords from the 8@5 branch to the 8@0 branch.

### C to Em
C -> Em has both superset and subset progressions.
* C=`0 4 7` and Em=`4 7 11` are both contained by 8@0 (and by its supersets 24@0 and 24@7), so the superset principle applies.
* As a subset move, C's 24@7 and Em's 15@6 share the bridge 18@9 (24@7->18@9->15@6).

The simplest reading is the superset principle within lcm 8, exactly as for the major triads in C F G. 
The difference in feel could be that where C is the triad 4@0, Em=`4 7 11` is not itself a small LCM family (subset of 8@0 / 24@0) so the minor chord has no fundamental of its own within the shared pattern.

### C to B
C -> B has only subset progressions.
* The pragmatic path is C's 24@7 to B's 15@6 through the shared bridge 18@9 (24@7->18@9->15@6). This is the harmonic minor move.
* C is also contained by 15@11, sharing 5@11 with 15@6, but key 7 of C is the collapsing key of 15@11 so it is unclear if this path is actually used - i.e. does the brain infer progressions using unstable placements?

15@6 also contains Em=`4 7 11`, so B lands on the very placement we reached for C→Em — B is the minor-flavoured chord of that same 15@6 branch.

### C to Cm
C -> Cm has only subset progressions.
* Among the origins we see 8@5, 15@4,7, 18@7, 24@0,5 - this is basically the 8@5 extended placement graph with 8@5, 15@4, 18@7, 24@0,5. 
The exception is 15@7 belonging to 8@8 - if 15@7 was stable with key 3 then this progression would be a superset progression.
* Among the targets we see 8@8, 15@2, 18@5,10, 24@3,8,10 - this is basically the 8@8 extended placement graph with 8@8, 18@10, 24@3,8.
The exception is 15@2, 18@5 and 24@10 belonging to the @3 branch above 4@3.

From this it looks like the progression could be a superset progression within 15@7, but is blocked by the unstable target placement.
Instead it looks like movement from 15@7 to 15@2 via 4@3, and indeed it does sound nice with a mel@0 (15@2 based) finish.
This is similar to the intro example of C -> Eb (4@3) where we could use 15@7 as a superset bridge to land in 18@10.
