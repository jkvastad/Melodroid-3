# Tiling a Song

The [progression principles](/docs/music/progression-principles) gave us legal moves between
chords: the **superset** move (two chords share one placement) and the **subset** move (two
placements are bridged by a shared subset). A *tiling* applies those moves to a whole piece:
divide the song into discrete pulses, classify the notes sounding in each pulse, and read off an
unbroken chain of progressions. Because popular music has been empirically validated as "good" by
many listeners, how cleanly a schema tiles a real song is evidence for the schema.

Let's look at Beethoven's *Für Elise*, as a POC, analysed with the
[`analyze midi`](/docs/cli/reference) command:

```
analyze midi --input output/Fur-Elise-Simple.mid \
  --collapse-isomorphic --pool-maximal --placements --stable-placements \
  --segments --segment-bridges --name-composites --max-chunks 600
```

## Chunks, segments, bridges

The command builds three nested granularities.

- **Chunks** — the song is quantised onto an onset grid; each non-empty slot is a chunk whose
  harmonic identity is just the set of sounding pitch classes (reference-free, folded mod 12).
  `--merge-repeats` folds time-adjacent slots with an identical key-set into one chunk, so a chunk
  is "where the harmony changes".
- **Segments** — a greedy partition of the chunk timeline into the *fewest* contiguous runs, each
  explainable by one shared label. Extend a run while the running intersection of the per-chunk
  label sets stays non-empty, then cut. A segment is therefore the longest unbroken stretch a
  single label survives across.
- **Bridges** — for each consecutive pair (of chunks, or of segments) the
  [progression](/docs/music/progression-principles) category (superset / subset / both / neither)
  and the bridges realising it.

The label can be either a **placement** (`{Lcm}@{At}`) or a **named scale** (`hm@9` = harmonic
minor anchored on key 9, etc.), giving two parallel tilings of the same song.

## Two layers

Those two labellings are not just two display options — they are two **layers** of the same
harmony, and the whole point of a full tiling is how they fit together.

- **Placement layer.** A state is a single placement. The moves are the two principles: a superset
  move holds inside one placement, a subset move steps `P_A → S → P_B` through a shared subset.
- **Scale layer.** A state is a *composite scale*. As derived in
  [composite scales](/docs/music/progression-principles), a composite scale is nothing but the
  key-union `P_A ∪ P_B` of a distinct placement pair joined through a shared subset `S`. The names
  that fall out of this construction — major, harmonic minor, harmonic major, melodic minor, bebop
  dominant, blues — are *derived* labels; their agreement with the traditional scale names is
  corroboration, not the reason they appear.

## The duality: a subset move *is* a composite scale

The two layers are tied by one identity:

> A **subset move** in the placement layer and a **composite scale** in the scale layer are the same
> object seen two ways — motion (`P_A → S → P_B`) versus a frozen set (`P_A ∪ P_B`).

The superset move is the degenerate case: both chords already live in one placement, so nothing is
bridged and there is no scale to name — it is "stay". Every genuine change of state is a subset
move, and every subset move names the composite scale you have modulated through. This is why a well
tiled passage reads simultaneously as a walk in the placement graph and as a short list of scales:
they are the same walk.

## Escalation at the gaps

Not every transition is a placement move. When neither principle applies — category **neither** —
the placement graph has *no edge*: no placement contains both chords, and no distinct containing
placements share a subset. These gaps are exactly where the tiling must **escalate** from the
placement layer to the scale layer.

The paradigm is the symmetric chord. As shown under
[symmetric chords and scale cores](/docs/music/progression-principles), dim7 `{0 3 6 9}` is
contained by *no* placement, yet it is the shared core of harmonic minor and harmonic major. At a
dim7 pulse the placement layer is genuinely silent; the scale layer carries the modulation until a
placement re-establishes. So a **neither** in the placement bridges is not a failure to report — it
is the signal "a scale is required here", and the scale that contains the offending chord as a core
*is* the bridge.

## Reading Für Elise

### Placement segments bridge cleanly, then stall on dim7

The opening theme tiles into short placement segments that bridge almost entirely by the subset
principle, naming a small vocabulary of composite scales:

```
seg → seg   Move                              Category  Composite
seg6→seg7   3 4 11 → 0 2 4 8 9 11             Subset    hM@4
seg7→seg8   0 2 4 8 9 11 → 0 2 4 5 7 9 11     Subset    hm@9
seg8→seg9   0 2 4 5 7 9 11 → 3 4 11           Subset    24@0·5@11·15@6
```

The theme is a near-periodic shuttle between **hM@4** and **hm@9** composites — the harmonic-major
and harmonic-minor colours of the piece — exactly the alternation you hear. A composite whose union
matches no catalog scale (the `seg8→seg9` row) keeps its full `P_A·S·P_B` constituents rather than a
bare key set, so you can still read which placements it is made of.

Around beats 123–157 the music reaches its agitated passage, and no placement bridges the move — so
the placement layer **escalates** and names the scale that carries it (the `↑` mark; the category
stays **neither** because the placement graph genuinely has no edge):

```
seg → seg   Move                  Category  Composite
seg33→seg34 0 2 4 5 9 11 → 0 3 6 9  Neither   ↑hm@1, ↑hm@4, ↑hm@7, ↑hm@10, ↑hM@1, ↑hM@4, ↑hM@7, ↑hM@10 …(+4)
seg34→seg35 0 3 6 9 → 0 3 9         Neither   ↑hm@1, ↑hm@4, ↑hm@7, ↑hm@10, ↑hM@1, ↑hM@4, ↑hM@7, ↑hM@10 …(+4)
```

`0 3 6 9` is dim7 — contained by no placement, so the placement layer reports **neither**, but the
Composite column now names the scales that host the move: all the harmonic minor / major scales dim7
is the core of (its fourfold symmetry means all four roots `{1,4,7,10}` appear for both), with the
`…(+4)` overflow folding in the remaining hosts. Reading the *scale* segmentation over the same
beats, the passage is indeed tiled by harmonic-minor scales — the blank is gone, and the escalation
reads directly in the placement table (compare the `dim7@4 → hm@2` player in the
[scale cores](/docs/music/progression-principles) section). The escalation rule prefers hosts over
the whole origin ∪ target, falling back to hosts over the arrival when — as with the symmetric dim7
— nothing hosts both.

Crucially, escalation draws from the **same** composite universe as the Composite column, not just the
six named scales: a named host reads `↑{Short}@{Anchor}`, an unnamed one keeps its `↑P_A·S·P_B`
constituents. A non-symmetric gap shows the mix directly — e.g. `seg35→seg36`
(`0 3 9 → 0 2 4 8 9 11`, Neither) escalates to `↑hm@9, ↑mm@9, ↑15@11·5@11·24@2, ↑15@11·5@4·24@5,
↑24@7·5@6·24@9`: two named harmonic scales followed by three unnamed composites that equally host the
move.

### Scale-segment bridges read through constituents

Unioning a whole (long) scale segment's chunks pools a phrase of melody into a chromatic blob no
placement contains, so a naïve union-then-test reads almost every scale-segment pair as **neither**.
Instead each segment's witness scale is decomposed into its constituent placements (`hm@9 =
15@11·18@2·24@0`), and the bridge is a placement-graph relation between the two segments' pooled
constituents — a shared placement (**pivot**, in the Superset column) and/or a subset-`S` join:

```
seg → seg   Move                          Category  Superset          Composite
seg17→seg18 0 3 4 8 9 11 → 0 2 4 8 9 11    Both      15@11             hm@4, hm@9, mm@9, hM@4, 15@6·5@11·24@0
seg18→seg19 0 2 4 8 9 11 → 0 2 4 5 7 9 11  Both      18@2, 24@0, 24@7  hm@9, mm@9, bb@0, bb@7, 15@11·5@4·24@5, 24@7·5@4·24@5
```

A scale-to-scale move now reads as a move in the placement graph between the two scales'
constituents — the natural dual of a placement bridge — with the shared pivot placement named
explicitly. (The Composite column still lists the composite scales of the crossing constituent pairs;
pairs whose union is no catalog scale keep their full `P_A·S·P_B` constituents.)

## The two layers, closed

With both edges in place the two layers close into a single tiling: placements wherever the graph
has an edge (superset = hold, subset = move named by its composite scale), escalating to a named
scale exactly at the gaps (the symmetric cores dim7 / aug, and anything no placement covers), and —
in the scale layer — every scale-to-scale move read back as a placement-graph move between
constituents. Every move is legible in both layers at once.
